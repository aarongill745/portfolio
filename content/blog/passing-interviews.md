# It's painful

I'll never miss the experience of being a final year uni student with no grad offer in hand. 

--- 

## Stage 1: It's you vs the computer

The first few parts of most interviews are automated, specially designed to weed out as many people as possible. 

You won't interact with a single human here. 

These machines are designed to pick up on particular key words and behaviours, usually based on the company's core values.

---

### **The Resume Screen**

The resume screen is the front door; there's no point in preparing for what's inside if you're not allowed to enter.

Before you even think about applying, that resume needs to be polished. Companies automatically filter resumes with ATS, machines love *buzxwords*.

Don't stray too far from the standard format. If a machine can't read your resume, it won't:

**Education**: University, Grade, Degree, date started, and graduated (projected graduation if you haven't yet)

**Experience**: Internships go here.

**Projects**: Personal projects, make sure they're interesting

**Skills**: Languages, frameworks, and skills that you have. It's a good idea to list everything since there are keywords that ATS will be looking for.

**Always submit as PDF**

### **The psychometric test**

These are the worst. I never thought I'd see general ability tests again after year 6, but here we are.

There are usually two sections: **Pattern recognition** (visual puzzles like "What's the next shape in the sequence?") and **personality scales** (you're presented with statements and pick where you fall on a scale). 

There's not much you can do to prepare for these—just be honest and try to stay consistent in your answers. Do a few psychometrics for companies that you're not too keen on first to get yourself familiar.

### **The "Video Interview"**

I put this in quotes because you're not actually talking to anyone. You're being recorded answering questions that pop up on your screen, usually through software like HireVue. 

Your responses are marked using AI, even your physical delivery (gestures, expression, etc), eye contact, tone, and even lighting are all a part of the marking.

---

## Stage 2: **Finally, a human**

Making it past the automated stage is impressive. Now, you finally get to meet some real humans.

### **The behavioral interview**

"Tell me about a time when..."

The STAR format is king in behavioral rounds. It's tried and tested.

If you don't know, STAR stands for:
- **Situation:** What's going on?
- **Task:** What do you have to do?
- **Action:** What did you do? How did you do it?
- **Result:** What was the result?

Expect to be asked follow-up questions, and don't lie, it's obvious when you're lying.

---

### **For big tech**

If you're interviewing at big tech, technical skill and design is the focus.

These can also show up in stage 1 (HackerRank or Codility). 

**Technical interviews** are usually what computer sci students focus most of their time on. Questions are usually LeetCode-style DSA problems. Fortunately, technical interviews are the most documented part of the entire process.

You will need to spend time learning and grinding all the different topics that can show up. If you're new to these, check out the [Neetcode 150](https://neetcode.io/).

**System design** comes in two flavors:

- **Low-level design:** 

You'll be asked to design classes and methods for a particular problem, testing your knowledge of OOP patterns and principles. Think "Design a library system."

- **High-level system design:** 

Less common for grad roles, but common for mid-level. You'll design the architecture for a given problem. "Design Dropbox," "Design a parking reservation system," etc. 

I found [Hello Interview](https://www.hellointerview.com/) to be a pretty good resource (not sponsored).

### **For non-tech companies (the big banks)**

I put big banks here, truth is that the market in Australia is so small that there's not much else.

Big banks don't focus on technical skills nearly as much as big tech. It's all about personality and culture fit. Company values are very important, read up on them and make sure you take them to heart when you go into those rounds.

**The assessment center (AC).** Instead of technical rounds and system design, we get to experience the joy of an assessment center. Often a group interview other applicants and an interviewer. You'll work through a case study-style problem **as a group.**

Here's the key: the AC isn't about coming up with the best solution or coming up with all the idea. It's about how well you work in a group. Focus on teamwork, contribute your own ideas, but also support others, and keeping track of pace and timing.

### **Smaller companies/Startups**

I found that a lot of the smaller-scale companies and startups copy big tech. Interviewers are usually a lot more senior though; you might even get an interview with the CTO.

---

## **The offer**

There's nothing to say here; you've made it.

The only thing I'd say here is that it's not that bad of an idea to keep interviewing and applying. You might get something better, and you'll be more confident knowing that you've got something to fall back on.

---

## **Conclusion**

Grad recruitment season is rough, and I don't envy anyone going through it. The market is tight, and it is really bad right now.
