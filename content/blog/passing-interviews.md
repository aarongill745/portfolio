# It's painful

I know the pain, you're in your final year, no grad role lined up, market's cooked, and you need all the help you can get.

You're in the dog house now...

---

## Stage 1: It's you vs the computer

The early stages are usually fully automated, designed to weed out as many candidates as possible. You won't interact with a single human here.

---

### **The Resume Screen**

Without passing the resume screen, you're not getting anywhere. 

Most companies use ATS to automatically filter resumes. First order of business is making your resume ATS friendly. You can find advice for this all over the net.

The next thing is formatting, what goes where.

A standard  format might go something like this:

**Education**: University, Grade, Degree, date started and graduated (projected gradation if you haven't yet)

**Experience**: Internships go here.

**Projects**: Personal projects, make sure they're interesting

**Skills**: Languages, frameworks, and skills that you have. It's a good idea to list everything since there are keywords ATS will be looking for.


### **The psychometric test**

These are the worst. I never thought I'd see general ability tests again after year 6, but here we are.

There are usually two sections: **Pattern recognition** (visual puzzles like "What's the next shape in the sequence?") and **personality scales** (you're presented with statements and pick where you fall on a scale). There's not much you can do to prepare for these—just be honest and try to stay consistent in your answers.

### **The "Video Interview"**

I put this in quotes because you're not actually talking to anyone. You're being recorded answering questions that pop up on your screen, usually through software like HireVue. Your responses are marked using AI, which means you need to work on your physical delivery—maintain eye contact with the camera, speak clearly, and appear engaged.

---

## Stage 2: **Finally, a human**

Making it past the automated stage is actually really impressive, now you'll have to impress real humans.

### **The behavioral interview**

The STAR format is king in behavioral rounds. It's tried and tested.

If you don't know, STAR stands for:
- **Situation:** What's going on?
- **Task:** What do you have to do?
- **Action:** What did you do? How did you do it?
- **Result:** What was the result?

STAR should be your bread and butter here. Expect to be asked follow up questions, it's pretty obvious when you're lying.

---

### **For big tech companies**

If you're interviewing at big tech, the focus is going to be on technical and design rounds.

**Technical interviews** are usually what comp sci students focus most of their time on. They also show up in stage 1 (via HackerRank or codility). Questions are usually LeetCode-style DSA problems. Fortunately, technical interviews are the most documented part of the entire process—entire question banks are available online. If you're new to these, check out the [Neetcode 150](https://neetcode.io/).

**System design** comes in two flavors:

- **Low-level design:** 

You'll be asked to design classes and methods for a particular problem, testing your knowledge of OOP patterns and principles. Think "Design a library system."

- **High-level system design:** 

Less common for grad roles, but common for mid-level. You'll design the architecture for a given problem. "Design Dropbox," "Design a parking reservation system," etc. I used [Hello Interview](https://www.hellointerview.com/) for these (not sponsored).

### **For non-tech companies (the big banks)**

Big banks don't focus on technical skills nearly as much as big tech. Here, it's all about personality and culture fit. You'll still see behavioral interviews, but you'll also face:

**The assessment center (AC).** Instead of technical rounds and system design, you get the assessment center. This is often a group interview with a few other applicants and one or more interviewers. You'll work through a case study-style problem **as a group.**

Here's the key: the AC isn't about coming up with the best solution to the problem. It's about how well you work in a group. Focus on teamwork, contributing your own ideas, supporting others' ideas, and keeping track of pace and timing.

### **Smaller companies/Startups**

I found that a lot of the smaller scale companies and startups model their interviewing process the same as big tech. The one difference is that the interviewers are usually a lot more senior, you might even get an interview with the CTO.

## **The offer**

There's nothing to say here, you've made it.

The only thing I'd say here is that it's not that bad of an idea to keep interviewing and applying. You might get something better, and you'll be more confident knowing that you've got something to fall back on.

Grad recruitment season is rough, and I don't envy anyone going through it. The market is tight is really bad right now.
