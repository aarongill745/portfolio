# It's painful

I know the pain, you're in your final year, no grad role lined up, market's cooked, and you need all the help you can get.

You're in the dog house now...

---

## Stage 1: It's just you and that screen

Everything here is online. You won't interact with a single human from the company you're applying to, it's all automated screening designed to weed out the thousands of applicants.

---

### **The resume screen**

Without passing the resume screen, you're not getting anywhere. 

Most companies use ATS to automatically filter resumes. First order of business is making your resume ATS friendly.

Search for "ATS friendly resume templates" to see what this looks like. Here's the standard format you should follow:

* **Education at the top.** You're applying to grad roles, so you need to make it very obvious that you're (A) a graduate, and (B) meet the minimum grade requirement.

* **Professional experience.** List any internships, professional work, or university development initiatives you've been part of. If you don't have any professional development experience (I didn't), you can list casual work instead—I included tutoring and my job at Coles to fill out space. BUT, I moved this section below my personal projects because stacking shelves and tutoring aren't as relevant for a software engineering role.

* **Personal projects.** Uni projects should be a last resort. Your personal projects don't have to be amazing full-stack applications with entire cloud infrastructures. Ever thought about making browser extensions or VS Code extensions that do something useful? Those count, and they show initiative.

* **Skills.** List every language, framework, and tool you know. These are buzzwords that automated resume screening will pick up on, so make sure you list everything relevant.

### The psychometric test

These are the worst. I never thought I'd see general ability tests again after year 6, but here we are.

There are usually two sections: **Pattern recognition** (visual puzzles like "What's the next shape in the sequence?") and **personality scales** (you're presented with statements and pick where you fall on a scale). There's not much you can do to prepare for these—just be honest and try to stay consistent in your answers.

### The "video interview"

I put this in quotes because you're not actually talking to anyone. You're being recorded answering questions that pop up on your screen, usually through software like HireVue. Your responses are marked using AI, which means you need to work on your physical delivery—maintain eye contact with the camera, speak clearly, and appear engaged.

## Stage 2: Finally, a human

If you make it through the automated gauntlet, congratulations! You'll now get to talk to actual people.

### The behavioral interview

**STAR! STAR! STAR!** The STAR format is king in behavioral rounds. Interviewers are explicitly connecting your stories to this framework, so you should too.

If you don't know, STAR stands for:
- **Situation:** What's going on?
- **Task:** What do you have to do?
- **Action:** What did you do? How did you do it?
- **Result:** What was the result?

STAR should be your bread and butter here. Don't bother doing anything fancy—just tell clear, structured stories that demonstrate your skills and experience.

---

### For big tech companies

If you're interviewing at big tech, you'll likely face technical and design rounds.

**Technical interviews** are very common. You'll see these in online assessments (via HackerRank or similar) and in face-to-face interviews. Questions are almost always LeetCode-style DSA problems. Fortunately, technical interviews are the most documented part of the entire process—entire question banks are available online. If you're new to these, check out the [Neetcode 150](https://neetcode.io/).

**System design** comes in two flavors:

*Low-level design:* You'll be asked to design classes and methods for a particular problem, testing your knowledge of OOP patterns and principles. Think "Design a library system."

*High-level system design:* Less common for grad roles, but increasingly common for mid-level. You'll design the architecture for a given problem—"Design Dropbox," "Design a parking reservation system," etc. I highly recommend [Hello Interview](https://www.hellointerview.com/) for these (not sponsored, just genuinely helpful).

### For non-tech companies (the big banks)

Big banks don't focus on technical skills nearly as much as big tech. Here, it's all about personality and culture fit. You'll still see behavioral interviews, but you'll also face:

**The assessment center (AC).** Instead of technical rounds and system design, you get the assessment center. This is often a group interview with a few other applicants and one or more interviewers. You'll work through a case study-style problem **as a group.**

Here's the key: the AC isn't about coming up with the best solution to the problem. It's about how well you work in a group. Focus on teamwork, contributing your own ideas, supporting others' ideas, and keeping track of pace and timing.

---

## The offer

Hopefully, you can get an offer—maybe even more than one. Once you get that first offer, you lose that chip on your shoulder. The pressure drops significantly.

It's actually not a bad idea to keep interviewing even after getting an offer, just in case you can get something better. You'll go into those interviews a lot more confident because the stakes aren't as high anymore.

---

## Conclusion

Grad recruitment season is rough, and I don't envy anyone going through it. The market is tight, the competition is fierce, and the process feels dehumanizing at times. But the only thing you're in control of is yourself—be prepared and give yourself the best chance possible. Good luck out there.
